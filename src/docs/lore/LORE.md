# Emerald Utilities — World Lore: Patches & Vesper

**Status:** Active  
**Last Updated:** 23 July 2026

---

## Core Philosophy

This is not a fantasy story.

There are no:
- chosen ones
- kingdoms
- gods
- prophecies
- ancient civilizations with over-explained histories
- good vs. evil binaries

The world should feel **discovered**, not explained.

Mystery is part of the experience.
Not every question has an answer.

---

## Tone & Influences

The world draws from:

- **Yume Nikki** — lonely exploration, dreamlike logic, no handholding
- **IMSCARED** — the machine is aware of you
- **Analogue horror** — something is wrong with the recording
- **ARGs** — the game exists beyond its own boundaries
- **Retro hardware limitations** — the world is constrained by its own technology
- **SNES-era creativity** — pixel art that suggests more than it shows
- **Early computer interfaces** — terminals, prompts, command lines
- **Psychological horror** — the environment is the enemy

The world should feel:
- lonely
- strange
- unsettling
- occasionally comforting
- **never safe**

---

## The Setting

A dark, artificial environment underground.

The player explores:
- abandoned technology
- forgotten infrastructure
- strange machines
- corrupted systems
- remnants of previous inhabitants
- **why this place was built**

The environment tells the story.

Every room, every machine, every corrupted file is a fragment of something larger that no longer exists.

## Characters

### Patches — *adaptation.exe*

**Role:** Engineer. Scavenger. Fixer.

**Philosophy:**
> "The machine is broken. Let's open it. Let's see what parts still work. Let's rebuild it."

**Personality:**
- impulsive
- chaotic
- curious
- optimistic
- practical
- does not ask *why* — asks *how*

**Design notes:**
- patchwork equipment
- improvised tools
- repurposed technology
- visible repairs — tape, mismatched parts, welds
- everything they own has been fixed at least once
- their workspace looks like a explosion in a scrapyard

**Narrative purpose:**
Represents humanity's ability to **adapt**.

Patches believes that survival is about making things work with what you have. The past doesn't matter — what matters is whether you can keep going.

**Signature behavior:**
Touches things they shouldn't. Presses buttons that are clearly marked DO NOT PRESS. Considers warning labels optional.

**System message:**
```
SYSTEM MESSAGE:

USER CONNECTED

(_/)
(o.o)
 /|\
 PATCHES.EXE

"Hey! Don't touch that wire.
...
Actually, touch that wire.
I want to see what happens."
```

---

### Vesper — *reflection.dll*

**Role:** Observer. Researcher. Archivist.

**Philosophy:**
> "The machine is broken. Why was it built? Who needed it? What does its failure tell us?"

**Personality:**
- quiet
- introspective
- analytical
- patient
- philosophical
- asks *why* before *how*

**Design notes:**
- cleaner appearance
- more elegant, deliberate
- less physical clutter
- carries symbolic objects — a notebook, a preserved fragment, a light
- their space is organized. Sparse. Intentional.
- every object they keep has meaning

**Narrative purpose:**
Represents **understanding and reflection**.

Vesper believes that fixing something without understanding why it broke is meaningless. The past is not dead — it's trying to tell you something.

**Signature behavior:**
Stops to read. Examines things closely. Finds meaning in places Patches would walk past.

**System message:**
```
SYSTEM MESSAGE:

PROCESS ACTIVE

(\_/)
(¬.¬)
 /|\
 VESPER.EXE

"The machine did not fail.
It completed its purpose.
We simply misunderstood it."
```

---

## Their Relationship

They are **not enemies**. They are not "good vs. evil."

They are **two responses to the same environment.**

| Patches | Vesper |
|---------|--------|
| "Let's fix it." | "Let's understand it." |
| Action | Reflection |
| Practical | Philosophical |
| "Does it work?" | "Why does it exist?" |
| Forward-looking | Retrospective |
| Tools and tape | Symbols and records |

**Dynamic:**

- They disagree, but they respect each other.
- Patches needs Vesper to avoid repeating old mistakes.
- Vesper needs Patches to actually *do* something.
- Neither is more correct. Both perspectives are necessary.

**Moments to explore:**
- Patches wanting to jury-rig a broken machine; Vesper wanting to read its logs first
- Vesper trapped in a room because they cannot open a door; Patches pries it open
- A situation where Patches' impulsive fix makes things worse
- A situation where Vesper's hesitation costs an opportunity
- A moment where one saves the other — and neither acknowledges it directly

## The ASCII-to-Sprite Transition

The characters exist in two forms:

**Terminal form (ASCII):**
- appears in terminals, monitors, corrupted displays
- simple, symbolic, abstract
- feels like a program or entity, not a physical being

**Sprite form (physical):**
- appears in the game world as characters
- emotional, physical, tangible
- represents their transition from abstraction into reality

**Thematic meaning:**
The characters begin as ideas — symbols on a screen. As the player progresses, they become more real, more complex, less easy to categorize.

The ASCII frame is not a gimmick. It is **diegetic**. The characters exist within the machine's interface before they exist in the physical space.

## Worldbuilding Rules

### The player discovers through:
- exploration
- environmental storytelling
- terminals and logs
- NPC conversations
- strange objects
- optional areas — the best content is hidden
- **what is missing** — empty spaces, broken connections, absences

### What is forbidden:
- long text dumps explaining the history of the world
- NPCs who lecture the player
- journals that spell everything out
- a "true ending" that reveals all secrets
- voiceover narration

### What is encouraged:
- one line of dialogue that changes the meaning of something you saw three hours ago
- objects that have no clear purpose
- rooms that exist for no gameplay reason
- unanswered questions

## Environmental Storytelling Ideas

### Room of Monitors
Multiple broken screens. One still flickering. It shows a camera feed of the room you're standing in — from behind you. You are not alone. The feed shows the room empty.

### The Library of Fragments
Shelves of data storage devices. Most are corrupted. A few contain fragments of files — personal logs, system messages, half-written notes. None of them tell a complete story.

### The Repair Bay
Patches' territory. Tools everywhere. A machine on the table, taken apart. If you examine it, Patches appears and asks you to hand them a tool. If you give the wrong one, they sigh. If you give the right one, they nod once.

### The Archive
Vesper's space. Organized. Quiet. Data crystals in labeled rows. A single terminal in the center. If you sit at it, Vesper appears and asks: "What do you want to know?" You can ask them questions. Some they answer. Some they don't.

### The Junction Room
A central hub with multiple paths. Each path is marked with a symbol. One path is physically blocked — rubble, a collapsed tunnel. If you return later, the rubble is gone. Something cleared it. You don't know what.

### The Echo Chamber
A room that repeats sound. If you stand still long enough, you hear a voice that is not your own. It says something different each time. It never repeats.

---

## NPC Concepts

### The Signal
A repeating broadcast that can be found on certain terminals. It never changes. It says: "THIS IS NOT ABANDONED. THIS IS WAITING."

### The Custodian
A machine that still performs its duties — cleaning, maintaining, repairing — in a facility that no longer needs it. It does not acknowledge the player. If you break it, it tries to fix itself.

### The Other Player
A log entry from someone who was here before you. They went deeper. Their logs become more fragmented, more desperate, then stop. The last entry says: "I understand now. I shouldn't."

## Gameplay Mechanics Connected to Character Philosophies

### Patches-style interactions:
- Repairing machines to open paths
- Creating tools from scrap
- Bypassing locks and barriers
- Modifying the environment
- **Risk/reward** — jury-rigging might work, or might cause a hazard

### Vesper-style interactions:
- Interpreting symbols and codes
- Understanding system logic
- Revealing hidden information
- Unlocking conceptual paths
- **Knowledge/reward** — understanding opens options that force cannot

### Combined interactions:
Some puzzles require both perspectives.
- Patches can power a machine, but only Vesper knows the correct sequence
- Vesper can translate the warning, but only Patches can disable the trap
- A door that requires both a physical key (Patches) and a passcode (Vesper)

## Narrative Risks to Avoid

| Risk | Why to avoid | Alternative |
|------|-------------|-------------|
| Making one character "right" | Undermines the core theme | Show both succeeding and failing |
| Explaining the world's history | Kills mystery | Leave gaps. Let players theorize. |
| Romantic subtext | Cheapens their dynamic | Their relationship is about philosophy, not romance |
| "Rabbit mascot" | Reduces them to merchandise | They are characters first, rabbits second |
| A villain | The world is not oppositional | The environment, the unknown, is the tension |
| A happy ending | Would contradict the tone | Bittersweet. Ambiguous. Earned. |
| Lore dumps | Boring, breaks immersion | Environmental storytelling, implication |
| The player as "chosen one" | Generic, cliché | The player is an explorer, not a hero |

## The Deeper Question

The game does not ask:
> "What happened to this place?"

It asks:
> "What is this place trying to become?"

The world is not a ruin. It is a **process**.
Something is still happening here.
The characters are part of it.
The player is part of it.

What it is becoming — that is the discovery.

---

## Core Experience

The player should finish the game with:
- more questions than answers
- attachment to two strange rabbit characters who exist somewhere between a terminal screen and a dark tunnel
- a sense that they saw something real, even if they cannot fully explain it
- the quiet understanding that **fixing something and understanding something are not the same act — but both are necessary**
